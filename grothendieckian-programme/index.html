Abstract, as published

The Grothendieckian Programme is a programme for machine reasoning about mathematics, set out in seven works of the WiseMotif Monograph of Mathematical Intelligence. This book is their overview, and it adds no mathematical result to them.

Present language models are trained to predict the next token. The seven works do not propose to replace them. They ask what any machine that reasons over local evidence must answer to, and what it would take for a machine to do what Grothendieck did with a hard problem: to build the setting in which the problem becomes natural, and to pose the next question there.

The answer is given as an architecture. At its centre, The Grothendieckian Machine (Series 01) holds mathematical worlds as typed, versioned and verifiable objects, with the status of every claim part of its type. Over it, Topos-Native Intelligence (Series 02) reasons inside a world as inside a topos: a context is a site, the evidence a sheaf, a conclusion a global section that exists only where the local data glue, and a failure to glue an obstruction that can be located. Around them, five research subjects (Series 03–07) — the Riemann Hypothesis and its generalisation, the conjecture of Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer, the Hodge conjecture, the abc conjecture and the Langlands programme — carry the method of recursive mathematical discovery through in full: a structure is proposed, tested against the cases in which the answer is known and abstracted where it survives, and the question is posed again; what fails is kept, as an obstruction, a gap or a test.

The book reads the seven works for what each of them changes — in every case the object rather than the statement — states the principles they share, and sets out the discipline of status labels, ledgers and falsification tests by which they are held. None of the seven works claims a proof of the problems it studies. Each says what it proves, what it reduces to stated hypotheses, and where it stops.

The architecture

Seven works carry the Programme, drawn as seven cells of one honeycomb. At the centre stands The Grothendieckian Machine (Series 01), which sets out how mathematical worlds are constructed, compared and carried from one setting to another, and how a machine may hold them as typed, versioned and verifiable objects. Above it, on a shared edge, stands Topos-Native Intelligence (Series 02), which states what an intelligence must be, internally, if it is to reason inside such worlds: reasoning native to a topos, in which a conclusion exists only where the local evidence glues. Around the two stand five research subjects — the Riemann Hypothesis and its generalisation, the conjecture of Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer, the Hodge conjecture, the abc conjecture and the Langlands programme — on each of which the method is carried through in full, with every step recorded in a form that a mathematician can check and a machine can execute.

The two meet at the site. A world of the first work presents its evidence as a site; the kernel of the second work places a sheaf of local information over that site and computes what glues. A bridge of the first work becomes a morphism of sites, and an obligation becomes an open cell of a contract, printed as open until it is discharged. What returns from the periphery to the core is method rather than result: a scoped archive of negative results that a search can consult before it proposes; the lesson that the measure of AI-assisted mathematics is theorems, and that an exact account of where they are missing is part of the mathematics; a record of each failure at the place where it occurred, so that the next recursion can begin from it.

The name

The adjective Grothendieckian names a mathematical method used in the seven works: the passage from objects to relations, relativity to a base, the replacement of an inadequate ambient category by a more expressive one, local-to-global passage by descent, and reconstruction from shadows. It carries no attribution of design or endorsement to Alexander Grothendieck, or to any other mathematician whose work the seven works cite, and no named person or institution has reviewed, authorised, sponsored or endorsed this publication. The dedication of the book expresses respect only.
