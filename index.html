Recursive Mathematical Discovery in the Age of Machine Reasoning

The Grothendieckian Programme is a programme for machine reasoning about mathematics, set out in seven works of the WiseMotif Monograph of Mathematical Intelligence. Series 00 is their overview, and it adds no mathematical result to them.

The question

A system trained to predict the next token can answer a remarkable range of questions. The Programme is concerned with a different capacity. Grothendieck's way with a hard problem was seldom to attack it where it stood: he built the setting in which the problem became natural, and let the solution come to it. The Programme asks whether a machine can take part in mathematics of that kind — whether it can construct a world in which a problem becomes natural, test that world against everything already known, say precisely where it fails, and pose the next question there — and what such a machine must be, internally, if anything it concludes is to be trusted.

The method

The Programme calls its method recursive mathematical discovery. A structure is proposed; it is tested against the cases in which the answer is known; what survives is abstracted into a new setting, and the question is posed again there. What does not survive is not discarded. It becomes a conjecture, a gap or a falsification test, with a statement, a status and a place in a ledger, and it is carried forward as part of the result.

The boundary

None of the seven works claims a proof of the problems it studies. The Riemann Hypothesis, the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, the Hodge conjecture and the abc conjecture remain open, and so does the Langlands correspondence over number fields beyond the cases the literature has proved; each work says so in its first pages, in a sentence of its own. What each offers is the state of the question after the method has been applied without concession: what has been proved, what has been reduced to stated hypotheses, and where each route stops.

The architecture

Seven works carry the Programme, drawn as seven cells of one honeycomb. At the centre stands The Grothendieckian Machine (Series 01), which sets out how mathematical worlds are constructed, compared and carried from one setting to another, and how a machine may hold them as typed, versioned and verifiable objects. Above it, on a shared edge, stands Topos-Native Intelligence (Series 02), which states what an intelligence must be, internally, if it is to reason inside such worlds: reasoning native to a topos, in which a conclusion exists only where the local evidence glues. Around the two stand five research subjects — the Riemann Hypothesis and its generalisation, the conjecture of Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer, the Hodge conjecture, the abc conjecture and the Langlands programme — on each of which the method is carried through in full, with every step recorded in a form that a mathematician can check and a machine can execute.

The two meet at the site. A world of the first work presents its evidence as a site; the kernel of the second work places a sheaf of local information over that site and computes what glues. A bridge of the first work becomes a morphism of sites, and an obligation becomes an open cell of a contract, printed as open until it is discharged. What returns from the periphery to the core is method rather than result: a scoped archive of negative results that a search can consult before it proposes; the lesson that the measure of AI-assisted mathematics is theorems, and that an exact account of where they are missing is part of the mathematics; a record of each failure at the place where it occurred, so that the next recursion can begin from it.
